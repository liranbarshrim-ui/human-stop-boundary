Approval
is not
refusal.

Most governance systems explain how continuation is managed. Very few define who is authorized to refuse irreversible continuation before execution proceeds.

Observation is not authority.
Escalation is not interruption.
Approval is not refusal.

No one refused.
So continuation
became automatic.

The structure contains oversight.
It does not contain refusal authority.

Organizations build review processes, escalation paths, and approval chains. These structures define who observed, who approved, who monitored.

They rarely define who, by name, is authorized to refuse continuation once institutional momentum already exists.

As responsibility distributes across functions and systems, refusal authority does not distribute with it.
It disappears.

Healthcare systems

What the structure shows.
What it conceals.

Underlying Reality
A human approved the workflow.
Approval became procedural rather than sovereign.
Escalation channels existed.
Escalation delayed responsibility instead of interrupting execution.
Everyone assumed oversight existed somewhere else.
Responsibility became structurally diffused.
The system technically allowed interruption.
The organizational cost of stopping made interruption unrealistic.
Multiple teams reviewed the decision.
Nobody had the authority to refuse continuation once momentum formed.
The question is never whether the system can continue.
The question is whether anyone inside it is authorized to refuse.

The question is not whether
people object.
The question is whether refusal exists structurally.

A human inside the process may not hold the authority to stop it. A committee is not a named individual. Oversight is not the power to interrupt.

A safeguard exists only when a specific individual is authorized to refuse — and named in advance.

Who, by name,
is authorized
to refuse?
