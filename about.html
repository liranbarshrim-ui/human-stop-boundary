A Structural Question

What this work
examines.
What it does not claim.

This work focuses on a single structural question: before a decision becomes irreversible, who, by name, is authorized to refuse it?

DAR functions as a mirror, not a mandate.
It does not determine whether an organization should stop.
It reveals whether authority to stop and authority to release continuation have been intentionally defined.

It does not evaluate people.
It ensures the question was asked before the event, not after it.
What is examined
How organizations define authority to interrupt continuation — and authority to release it once a recognized stop condition has appeared. And what the public record shows about what happens when that authority is absent, unnamed, or overridden.
How it proceeds
Through observation of publicly available post-incident records, regulatory findings, and documented governance failures. Each case is examined for the presence or absence of a named individual expected to refuse continuation before a decision became irreversible.
The method
This work begins from a simple assumption: terms that appear unified often contain multiple functions. Governance language frequently treats these functions as equivalent. The research proceeds by separating them and identifying the conditions under which they diverge.

Approval and refusal. Human review and stop authority. Escalation and interruption. Assigned authority and effective authority.

The objective is not to introduce new terminology. The objective is to identify where structurally important distinctions disappear.

This approach can be described as conceptual disaggregation: separating functions that are commonly treated as equivalent and examining where they diverge.
What is not claimed
DAR does not evaluate people. It ensures the question was asked before the event, not after it.

This work does not attribute fault or negligence to named individuals. It does not offer legal interpretation. It does not prescribe governance structure. It identifies a recurring structural pattern observable from the public record across multiple domains: the absence of a named individual authorized to release or refuse continuation.
The recurring question
Who, by name, was explicitly committed in advance to refuse continuation — before the decision became irreversible?

If that name does not exist before the event, the question cannot be answered after it.

Systems continue — and justify their own continuation — after the moment they should have stopped. Not because stopping was impossible. Because no one had been clearly assigned the authority to refuse.

DAR does not evaluate people.
It ensures the question was asked before the event, not after it.

If no one is named before the event, responsibility becomes interpretation after it.

The organization is not required to change.
But it can no longer claim the question was never asked.
