Decision Authority Research

What this work
examines — and
what it does not claim.

What is examined
The structural relationship between continuity architecture and interruption architecture in governance systems. Specifically: at what point does continuation cease to require explicit re-authorization from a named individual, and what does the public record show about that condition?
How it proceeds
Through observation of publicly available post-incident records, regulatory findings, and documented governance failures. Each case is examined for the presence or absence of a named individual with prior, explicit commitment to refuse continuation under uncertainty. No insider knowledge is claimed.
What is not claimed
This work does not attribute fault or negligence to named individuals. It does not offer legal interpretation. It does not claim to explain the internal decisions of any organization. It identifies a recurring structural pattern observable from the public record across multiple domains.
The recurring question
Who, by name, was explicitly committed in advance to refuse continuation — before the decision became irreversible? If that name does not exist before the event, the question cannot be answered after it.
Governance Observation Series
Three domains. Three timescales. The pattern observed across financial execution, software deployment, and safety certification.

This work begins where post-incident explanation begins — after continuation has already occurred, after the question of who could have stopped it has no answer that precedes the event. The research framing above describes the method. What follows is where the question came from.

A system can continue —
and justify
its own continuation.

The question did not begin as theory.

It came from watching systems continue — and justify their own continuation — after the moment they should have stopped. Not because stopping was impossible. Because no one had been clearly assigned the authority to stop it.

That gap — between observation
and named obligation —
is what this work examines.

This is not a legal institution. Not a regulatory body. Not a consultancy.

One person. No institution behind it. Just the question itself — and a body of observation that asks it across domains.

Most systems define who approves.
Almost none define who can refuse.

The question is not whether people object.

The question is whether refusal exists structurally — before the decision becomes irreversible.
