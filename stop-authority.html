Named Stop Authority

Why approval systems fail when no one can stop continuation at the critical moment.

Executive Signal
Approval Is Common
Named refusal is rare. Most organizations know how decisions proceed. Far fewer know exactly who can stop them.
When success happens, responsibility is shared.
When failure happens, it converges to one name.

Most governance systems were designed to answer one question: how do we proceed?

Far fewer were designed to answer the harder question: how do we stop?

Approval enables continuation. Stop authority interrupts continuation. They are not the same function.

The illusion of shared ownership

During normal operations, institutions prefer collective language:

Reviewed by stakeholders
Approved through governance channels
Passed all required stages
Aligned across teams

That language survives while outcomes remain positive.

When harm appears, it collapses.

Who was responsible?

At that moment, distributed ownership becomes personal accountability.

Stop authority is not policy

A stop function is not real because it appears in policy.

It is real only when a specifically identified person can make continuation impossible without explicit release.

If continuation remains the default under uncertainty, stopping was symbolic from the beginning.

If no answer exists by name, that is already an answer.
Why AI changes the stakes

Autonomous systems act at machine speed and global scale.

When systems move faster than institutions can interrupt them, speed itself becomes a governance risk.

The question is no longer only who owns failure.

It is also who owns the ability to stop.

Who is explicitly named to stop your next irreversible decision?
