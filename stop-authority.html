Named Refusal Authority

Who, by name,
is authorized
to say no?

Before the point of no return — not who reviewed it, not who escalated it. Who could stop it.

Approval exists.
Refusal authority often does not.
Why This Happens

Most organizations define clearly who approves. Procedures. Hierarchies. Escalation paths.

But approval is not refusal. A system can contain oversight, review, and sign-off — and still contain no named individual authorized to stop continuation before it becomes irreversible.

When nobody is explicitly required to refuse, continuation becomes the default. Not because everyone agreed. Because stopping required a name that was never assigned.

In practice, the missing authority is often not the authority to cancel.

It is the authority to say: "not yet."

Not yet because uncertainty remains unresolved.
Not yet because continuation may already be approaching the irreversible threshold.
Not yet until someone named can justify proceeding.
Observation is not authority.
Escalation does not create refusal authority.
Approval is not refusal.
The authority to say "not yet" is often the one nobody assigns.
Where It Appears
Financial Execution
A system moves faster than any individual can track. Everyone monitors. Nobody is named to stop.
Software Deployment
A release propagates across infrastructure. Approval existed. Named refusal authority did not.
AI Systems
Automated execution proceeds. Human oversight is present. Nobody is explicitly named to refuse continuation.
If the answer requires meetings, escalation chains, or interpretation — authority may not exist where it needs to.

The question is not whether oversight exists. The question is whether anyone, by name, is authorized to refuse continuation before it becomes irreversible — and whether that authority is practically available when time matters.

If this question is difficult to answer in your organization — that is where the conversation begins.
