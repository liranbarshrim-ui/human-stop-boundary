The Boeing
Question

The 737 MAX certification path extended across years of accumulated decisions. Each individual decision appeared bounded and revisable. The public record raises a structural question: at what point did the certification path cease to require explicit re-authorization under conditions of accumulating uncertainty?

This observation differs from the preceding cases in a significant way. Knight Capital and CrowdStrike involved acute failure events measurable in minutes. The Boeing MCAS governance question is structural and longitudinal: it concerns how a certification path accumulates irreversibility across time, such that no single decision appears to be the moment of commitment — even as the cumulative path becomes increasingly difficult to reverse.

01 Accumulation of decisions
2011
737 MAX program launched under competitive pressure
Boeing launches the 737 MAX to compete with Airbus A320neo. A key constraint: the aircraft must be certifiable as a derivative of the existing 737 family, not as a new type. This allows airlines to avoid full pilot retraining. The constraint shapes every subsequent design and certification decision.
2012–2016
MCAS developed to address aerodynamic behavior
New CFM LEAP engines require repositioning on the wing, altering the aircraft's handling characteristics at high angles of attack. MCAS — Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — is developed to compensate. The system is designed to activate automatically, without pilot awareness, to adjust the horizontal stabilizer. The decision to keep MCAS's existence undisclosed in pilot documentation is made during this period.
2016–2017
FAA delegates certification authority. MCAS authority expanded.
The FAA delegates significant portions of certification review to Boeing's own engineers under Organization Designation Authorization. Separately, MCAS's authority over the stabilizer is expanded — the system is given greater range of movement than originally assessed — while the public record does not clearly make visible a corresponding reassessment of its failure modes or pilot recovery time, or whether this expansion triggered additional regulatory review.
Oct 2018
Lion Air Flight 610. 189 lives lost.
MCAS activates based on a faulty angle-of-attack sensor reading. Pilots, unaware of the system's existence and not trained on its recovery procedure, are unable to counteract repeated nose-down commands. The aircraft enters the Java Sea 13 minutes after takeoff. The aircraft remains in service.
Mar 2019
Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302. 157 lives lost.
An identical failure sequence. MCAS activates on a faulty sensor. Pilots follow Boeing's updated emergency procedure — which proves insufficient against the system's authority. The aircraft crashes six minutes after takeoff. Regulators ground the 737 MAX globally. The aircraft does not return to service for 20 months.
02 The structural question

Each decision appeared bounded.
The path did not.

The Boeing MCAS case does not present a single moment of identifiable governance failure. It presents a sequence of decisions — competitive framing, design constraints, certification delegation, system expansion, documentation choices — each of which appeared manageable and revisable in isolation.

The structural question DAR observes is different: at what point did the cumulative path cease to require explicit re-authorization under conditions of accumulating uncertainty?

When each decision appears reversible, the path toward irreversibility has no formal encounter with refusal.

Each individual decision appeared reversible.
The certification path, as a whole, accumulated toward irreversibility.
The public record does not identify the point at which re-authorization was required.

This is what distinguishes the Boeing case from the preceding two. Knight Capital and CrowdStrike involved systems moving faster than human decision cycles. Boeing involved a certification path moving slowly enough that each decision appeared considered — while the cumulative trajectory approached a condition from which return became increasingly costly, and ultimately was not taken before loss of life.

03 What was present. What the record does not show.
What existed
  • FAA certification authority and delegation framework
  • Internal Boeing engineering review processes
  • Safety assessment documentation for MCAS
  • Regulatory communication channels between Boeing and FAA
  • Post-Lion Air internal awareness of MCAS behavior
  • Congressional oversight after grounding
What the public record does not make visible
  • A named individual with explicit authority to pause the certification path under accumulating uncertainty
  • A defined threshold at which MCAS authority expansion required independent re-authorization
  • A condition under which the derivative-type constraint required explicit re-examination
  • A named stop authority between Lion Air and Ethiopian Airlines

The public record makes visible governance structures oriented toward certification. It does not clearly make visible a named individual whose explicit authorization was required before the certification path could continue under accumulating uncertainty.

04 The pattern across three cases

Across three domains — financial execution, software deployment, and safety certification — a structural relationship appears in the public record. In each case, the system was organized around a path designed for continuity. In each case, the interruption condition was either absent, slower than the primary path, or not tied to a named individual with prior commitment.

The velocity differs. The structure does not. In each case, the path toward the irreversible outcome does not appear, in the public record, to have required the explicit authorization of a named individual before continuation proceeded. Oversight existed. Process existed. Named stop authority — visible in the public record as designated in advance — does not appear in any of the three cases.

The question this case leaves behind
At what point did the certification path require explicit re-authorization under accumulating uncertainty?

Not who approved each individual decision. Not which regulator signed the final certificate.

At what specific condition of accumulating uncertainty was a named individual required to explicitly release continuation — rather than having the path proceed by default toward the next decision?

If such a condition is not made visible within the certification architecture, the path toward irreversibility may have no formal encounter with refusal. It accumulates, decision by decision, until continuation no longer requires explicit reconsideration.
