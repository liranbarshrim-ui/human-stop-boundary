Who Still Sees Enough to Know?

On Visibility, Mediated Reality, and the Quiet Erosion of Meaningful Interruption

Liran Bar-Shrim  ·  May 2026

Governance depends not only on authority, but on visibility.

Without visibility, meaningful interruption becomes difficult to exercise.

Much attention has focused on whether meaningful interruption authority still exists in mediated systems.

A related question has gone largely unasked:

What happens when organizations no longer see clearly enough to know that stopping is necessary?

I

The Authority Was Never the Problem

Organizations rarely lose the formal authority to stop. Executives still retain approval power. Risk committees still retain escalation rights. Compliance officers still retain the authority to flag concern.

The question that receives less attention is different: does the person with authority to stop still see enough of the original situation to recognize when stopping is warranted?

In most governance discussions, this is assumed. Yet mediated systems may quietly weaken the assumption itself.

Decisions increasingly emerge through layers of mediation: summaries, retrieval systems, rankings, triage engines, generated recommendations. Each layer increases speed and consistency. Each layer may also narrow what remains visible enough to question before action hardens.

Human mediation leaves traces: hesitation, ambiguity, contradiction. Algorithmic mediation increasingly produces coherent outputs at every step. The coherence itself may conceal the compression.
II

How Visibility Quietly Narrows

The erosion rarely begins with dramatic failure. It begins with small, reasonable acts of compression.

A summary omits nuance. A retrieval system surfaces what appears most relevant. A ranking mechanism amplifies what has already received attention. A triage engine prioritizes signals that resemble prior patterns.

Each step appears reasonable in isolation. Each step may even improve efficiency. Yet over time, these steps compound.

Information that is consistently deprioritized does not disappear. It simply stops appearing in the version of reality the organization actually uses. The organization does not lose data. It loses the practical ability to encounter it.

What makes this difficult to govern is precisely that no obvious failure occurs. The system works. The outputs remain coherent. The picture reaching the person responsible is internally consistent. It is simply no longer the same picture that existed before the mediation began.

Mediated systems do not only filter what is seen. They may gradually redefine what counts as relevant in the first place.
III

The Moment That Passes Unnoticed

There is a specific moment mediated systems may quietly produce. Not a crisis. Not an obvious malfunction.

A moment in which an organization begins acting from a narrowed version of reality, while the person responsible has no clear signal that narrowing has occurred.

In many cases, confidence may even increase as visibility narrows. Mediated outputs often appear cleaner, more coherent, and easier to act upon than the uncertainty they replaced. The organization may feel increasingly informed precisely as it becomes less exposed to uncertainty.

In healthcare, a subtle but relevant symptom omitted from an early AI-generated summary may quietly disappear from subsequent records. Weeks later, clinical teams may find themselves making decisions from a patient history that is internally coherent, yet materially different from the original patient reality.

In financial compliance, risks repeatedly categorized as low priority may gradually fade from operational attention. Not because the risks disappeared, but because they stopped appearing in the mediated view the institution actually uses.

The human remained in the loop. The human approved. The human stayed accountable. The human simply no longer encountered enough of the original uncertainty to recognize that interruption was necessary.
IV

The Harder Problem: Not Knowing What You No Longer See

It is not only that organizations gradually see less. It is that they gradually lose the ability to know they are seeing less.

When mediated outputs are coherent, they do not announce their own incompleteness. A summary that omits nuance does not carry a warning. A retrieval that surfaces familiar patterns does not reveal what it left behind.

The gap between what the organization sees and what it no longer sees remains invisible precisely because the mediation that created it also shapes what the organization uses to evaluate its own picture of reality.

Adding review steps does not close the gap if the review operates on the same mediated outputs it is meant to check. An audit is only as independent as its access to non-mediated reality.

The signals most likely to require interruption are often statistical outliers — precisely the kind of information mediated systems are designed to smooth, rank lower, or treat as noise.

Training people to be more skeptical does not restore visibility that the system has already removed from view. The organization cannot see what it can no longer encounter.

V

The Harder Governance Question

Governance traditionally asks: Who is responsible? After failure, it asks: Who should have stopped it?

There is a quieter question organizations rarely ask in advance: does the person responsible still see clearly enough to know that stopping is necessary?

If organizations cannot easily detect loss of visibility from within the same mediated system shaping their perception, the problem becomes most difficult to recognize precisely when it matters most.

Governance may need to focus not only on decision quality, but also on preserving meaningful access to non-mediated reality.

Responsibility without visibility is not governance. It is the appearance of governance.

The deepest risk may not be that organizations lose the ability to stop. It may be that they lose the ability to know they needed to.

The deepest governance failure may not begin when organizations stop seeing. It may begin when they become most confident that they already have.
