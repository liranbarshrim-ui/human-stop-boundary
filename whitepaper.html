White Paper · 2026

Named Stop Authority
in High-Risk Decisions

A Practical Governance White Paper
Executive Summary

Modern organizations are increasingly optimized for speed. Yet many remain underprepared for one critical moment: when a high-risk decision must be stopped quickly, who has the clear authority to do so?

This paper proposes a practical governance principle: define Named Stop Authority in advance for a narrow class of consequential decisions. Not one name, but three. Each with a distinct and non-overlapping function.

The Hidden Governance Gap

Responsibility is often assigned before stopping authority is defined.

Many organizations maintain clear approval workflows, budget authority, reporting lines, and escalation structures. Who can interrupt momentum immediately when new uncertainty appears?

Where this is unclear, responsibility may later become personal while authority had previously been diffuse.

The Problem

Most systems know how to approve. Few know how to stop.

During crisis moments, multiple teams may be involved while no one clearly owns the stop decision.

Production incidents
Cyber events
AI deployments
Financial transactions
Operational emergencies
The Three Names

One practical model uses three distinct authorities.

A stop without a separate hold owner becomes a stop that dissolves. A hold without a separate release authority becomes an unowned delay. All three must be named before the decision begins.

01
Stop Authority
Named in advance. Empowered to halt execution when trigger conditions are met. Cannot be the release authority.
02
Hold Authority
Holds the decision in suspension while governance responds. Distinct from the stop authority. If the hold requires convening, it has already dissolved.
03
Release Authority
A third named individual. Empowered to resume the decision following documented review. Release requires written rationale.
Override Risk

When the stop was overridden informally.

The stop was invoked. Someone senior made a call, not formally, not in writing. The stop was real. The override was also real. By the time the outcome arrived, the person who stopped it was no longer relevant to the conversation.

When a stop authority is invoked and then overridden, the failure becomes personal. The person who stopped it has documentation. The person who overrode it often does not.
Escalation Failure

When no one responds.

A stop was invoked. The hold is active. The release authority was notified. No response followed. A stop that can dissolve in silence was never fully designed.

Every stop requires a named escalation path: one identified senior authority who must respond when the release owner fails to do so within a defined period. Silence is not a neutral outcome. It is a governance failure requiring documentation.

Cost Transfer Problem

When the warning was right and the cost fell on the person who warned.

A stop was invoked. The concern proved valid. Continuation proceeded anyway. If the cost of a correct stop is borne by the person who invoked it, future stops will not be invoked. The governance mechanism destroys itself through its own incentive structure.

If the person who warned correctly bears the cost of being right, the next warning will never arrive.
Oversight of Stop Authority

Stopping power must be fast. Its use must be reviewable.

A stop mechanism without oversight can become arbitrary veto power. An oversight mechanism without real stop power becomes symbolic process.

Immediate authority when triggers arise
Rapid secondary review
Written rationale
Escalation path
Board or audit visibility
Stop authority without review becomes veto.
Review without stop becomes theater.
Case Examples

The pattern is not theoretical.

In each of the following cases, the system continued because no one was named, in advance, to stop it.

Knight Capital Group — August 1, 2012 A software deployment error activated a dormant trading algorithm. In 45 minutes, Knight Capital executed millions of erroneous orders. Loss: $440 million. No single named individual held the authority to halt the trading system in real time. Operators observed anomalous behavior. The system continued executing while governance attempted to convene. The gap between observation and named stop authority cost $10 million per minute.
Boeing MCAS The MCAS system continued operating under conditions its pilots could not override through standard procedure. The governance question was not whether the system was approved. It was whether any named individual held the authority to halt it before outcomes became irreversible at scale.
In each case: the process was not absent. The name was.

The faster the system, the clearer the stop path must be.

Automation compresses time between signal and consequence. When AI removes the visible decision-maker, governance must restore the visible stop-maker. The question is not who reviews later. It is who can halt the system in time.

If no one is named to stop it, it should not run.
Why Boards Care

Governance exposure is personal before it is organizational.

Unclear ownership becomes visible after incidents, not before
Delayed stop response concentrates liability on whoever was most visible
Personal exposure follows when authority was diffuse and outcomes were harmful
Inability to demonstrate named oversight weakens board defensibility
Business Value

Named stop authority reduces unmanaged exposure.

Reduces unmanaged liability in high-speed decision environments
Shortens crisis response time by pre-assigning ownership
Clarifies escalation before pressure forces improvisation
Protects board oversight with documented authority structures
Creates a governance record reviewable after any incident
Closing

Strong organizations are not defined only by speed.

They are also defined by whether someone can legitimately and clearly say stop, and whether that word belongs to a name, not a process.

If no one is named to stop the irreversible, the organization has already delegated the decision to momentum.
