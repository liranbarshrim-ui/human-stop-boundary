White Paper · 2025

Named Stop Authority
in High-Risk Decisions

A Practical Governance White Paper
Executive Summary

Modern organizations are increasingly optimized for speed.

Yet many remain underprepared for one critical moment:

When a high-risk decision must be stopped quickly, who has the clear authority to do so?

This paper proposes a practical governance principle: define Named Stop Authority in advance for a narrow class of consequential decisions.

The Hidden Governance Gap

Responsibility is often assigned before stopping authority is defined.

Many organizations maintain clear approval workflows, budget authority, reporting lines, and escalation structures.

Who can interrupt momentum immediately when new uncertainty appears?

Where this is unclear, responsibility may later become personal while authority had previously been diffuse.

The Problem

Most systems know how to approve. Few know how to stop.

During crisis moments, multiple teams may be involved while no one clearly owns the stop decision.

Production incidents
Cyber events
AI deployments
Financial transactions
Operational emergencies
Exposure Logic

Failure often converges to one name.

Who signed?
Who knew?
Who could have stopped it?

If these are different people, accountability becomes structurally unstable.

The Principle

Named Stop Authority

A clearly identified individual is empowered to pause, halt, isolate, or escalate before damage expands.

Not a committee. Not a department. A named person.

Oversight of Stop Authority

Stopping power must be fast. Its use must be reviewable.

A stop mechanism without oversight can become arbitrary veto power.

An oversight mechanism without real stop power becomes symbolic process.

Immediate authority when triggers arise
Rapid secondary review
Written rationale
Escalation path
Board or audit visibility
Stop authority without review becomes veto.
Review without stop becomes theater.
Boards & Executives

Approval systems are commonly reviewed. Stop systems are not.

Boards frequently review controls, budgets, plans, and risk reports.

Far fewer review whether management can halt dangerous continuation under pressure.

AI & Autonomous Systems

The faster the system, the clearer the stop path must be.

Automation compresses time between signal and consequence.

Governance maturity increasingly includes interruption capability.

Closing

Strong organizations are not defined only by speed.

They are also defined by whether someone can legitimately and clearly say:

Stop.
