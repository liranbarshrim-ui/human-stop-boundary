Who Is Authorized
to Stop
the System?

On autonomous execution, accountability fragmentation,
and named interruption authority.

Opening Observation

As execution becomes increasingly autonomous, interruption authority becomes increasingly ambiguous.

Systems accelerate. Accountability fragments. Escalation exists. Named refusal often does not.

The question is not whether organizations intend to maintain control. Most do. The question is whether a named individual exists — before execution — whose explicit release is required before continuation proceeds under conditions of material uncertainty.

Governance Conditions

When these conditions are present,
interruption authority becomes structurally ambiguous.

01
Automated continuation without explicit release condition
02
Distributed approval structures with no named interruption authority
03
Escalation latency that exceeds the reversibility window
04
Override conditions that are undocumented or verbal
05
Continuation that proceeds without explicit release from a named individual
06
Refusal authority that is assumed rather than designated in advance
Illustrative Scenario

Uncertainty appeared.
Continuation proceeded.
Authority remained structurally unclear.

Model Release / Infrastructure Deployment
A deployment proceeds under standard release conditions. During propagation, anomalous behavior is detected. Alerts exist. Monitoring is active. Escalation channels are open.

No named individual holds prior, explicit commitment to refuse continuation before the deployment window closes. The escalation path reaches a committee. The committee convenes. By the time a decision is possible, the deployment has exceeded its reversibility threshold.

The question that follows — who was authorized to stop this — has no answer that precedes the event.
Automated Financial Execution
A trading system executes at speed. Position accumulation deviates from expected parameters. Risk systems flag the condition. Personnel observe it.

Continuation does not require explicit release. Stopping requires action. Action requires authority. Authority is distributed. Forty-five minutes later, the position is unrecoverable.

The record shows who observed. It does not show who was named in advance to stop.
Structural Observation

The governance problem is not only whether someone can intervene. It is whether interruption authority exists before continuation becomes operationally irreversible.

Oversight structures typically define who reviewed, who approved, who monitored. They rarely define who, by name, was committed in advance to refuse continuation under conditions of material uncertainty.

When that name does not exist before the event, it cannot be identified after it. The record shows process. It does not show authority.

In automated environments, responsibility can drift faster than authority is named.

Named Interruption Authority

What named interruption authority
requires.

Named in advance
A specific individual — not a function, not a committee — designated before execution begins.
Explicit trigger
A defined condition under which continuation requires explicit release before proceeding.
Escalation path
A named escalation authority who must respond if the primary authority does not act within a defined interval.
Override documentation
Any continuation that proceeds over a refusal must carry written rationale from a named authorization authority.
Prior commitment
The record of named authority exists before the event — not as a response to it.
Closing
The absence of interruption authority often becomes visible only after continuation occurs.

At that point, the question of who was authorized to stop it has no answer that precedes the event. Governance becomes visible at the moment continuation requires a name.
