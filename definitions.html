Decision Governance — Definitions

Core terms in decision authority.

These definitions are not standard industry terminology. They describe a structural reality that existing frameworks have not named precisely.

A working vocabulary for decision governance.

Each term addresses the same gap: the absence of a named individual with the obligation to stop.

01
Named Stop Authority

A single individual explicitly identified, in advance, as holding the obligation and authority to stop a specific irreversible decision before execution occurs.

Not a role. Not a team. A name — recorded before the decision moves.

The word "named" is not rhetorical. It means a specific person, identified by name, in writing, prior to execution. Anything less is not stop authority. It is the appearance of stop authority.
02
Irreversible Decision

A decision that, once executed, cannot be undone without disproportionate cost, harm, or loss. The point at which continuation and stopping are no longer equivalent choices.

Irreversibility is not binary. It is a threshold — and that threshold must be defined before it is reached.

A system can be technically correct and still execute an irreversible decision without anyone being required to stop it. Technical correctness and stop authority are separate questions.
03
Decision Authority Record (DAR)

A written record identifying the single named individual holding stop authority over a specific irreversible decision pathway, defined before execution begins.

The DAR does not approve the decision. It names who can stop it — and makes that person's obligation explicit, documented, and binding before the threshold is reached.

A DAR is not a policy document. It is a commitment that names a person and defines the conditions under which that person is obligated to refuse continuation.
04
Bound Refusal

The condition in which a named individual is formally committed, in advance, to refuse continuation of a decision pathway if specific conditions are met.

Bound refusal is the opposite of passive oversight. It requires the individual to act — to stop — not merely to observe and report.

Most governance structures create the appearance of oversight without creating bound refusal. The difference determines whether a stop is structurally possible at all.
05
Stop Authority Gap

The structural absence of a named individual explicitly empowered to stop an irreversible decision at the moment it must be stopped.

The gap is not a failure of process. It is a feature. Naming one person creates real accountability. Most systems are designed to avoid it.

When a stop authority gap exists, continuation does not require permission. It requires only that no one refuses. And without a named individual bound to refuse, refusal is optional — which means it rarely happens.

These terms describe a structural question that regulation rarely asks and governance frameworks rarely answer.

Documentation describes what should happen. Regulation asks what happened. DAR asks who was supposed to stop it before it happened.

Not a team. Not a function. A person. Named. Accountable. Committed in advance.
