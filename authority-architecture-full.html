Authority Architecture

A private framework for designing decision authority inside organizations before pressure, uncertainty, and momentum take control.
Core Principle

Before something goes wrong, someone must already be named to stop it.

Approval systems often exist. Stop systems often do not.

If no one is named to stop it,
momentum already has authority.
Authority Roles

Three functional roles

Stop

Immediate authority to halt continuation when thresholds are triggered.

Hold

Maintains suspension while review, escalation, or clarification occurs.

Release

Named authority who resumes continuation with documented rationale.

Mandatory Response Architecture

A hold without mandatory response is a slow override by silence.

Between Stop and Release there is a window. What happens inside that window determines whether the stop is a governance event or a delay that dissolves on its own.

Most organizations leave this window unowned. No one is required to respond. No clock is running. The hold persists until organizational pressure quietly resumes continuation without formal release.

Response window A defined maximum time before a named authority must formally respond to the stop. Not acknowledge. Respond. A response requires documented position.
Who must respond Named in advance. Not whoever is available. Not the stop authority themselves. A specific individual at a level above the stopped decision.
No response default If no response arrives within the window, the hold does not dissolve. It escalates automatically to the next named level. Silence is not consent. Silence triggers escalation.
Auto-escalation path Three levels named in advance: primary respondent, secondary, board-level. Each with a defined window. If all three fail to respond, the stop holds and the failure is documented as a governance event in itself.
Default state until release Hold remains active. Continuation is not available. No informal resumption. The stopped decision does not proceed because people stopped paying attention to it.
Silence is often how stop authority is defeated.
Not by override. By neglect.
Human Friction Layer

Why organizations don't stop even when they can

The structural mechanism may exist. The human conditions that allow it to function often do not.

KPI exposure The person with stop authority owns a number. Stopping means missing it. The authority exists on paper. The incentive structure cancels it in practice.
Career cost Stopping creates enemies. Continuing creates deniability. Ambitious people read this correctly and do not stop.
The room reads wrong Everyone in the meeting wants to proceed. The person who can stop feels the social weight. Silence is easier. Silence is not governance.
Informal override A senior leader signals displeasure after the stop. No formal override occurs. The stop authority quietly reverses position. The mechanism worked. The culture undid it.
Diffuse pressure No one orders the person to proceed. But three colleagues mention the deadline. Two mention the client. One mentions the board. Pressure without instruction is still pressure.
Punishment arrives late The person who stopped gets a smaller budget next quarter. No connection is stated. No connection needs to be stated. The signal is clear. The next person with stop authority reads it.
The mechanism that fails is rarely the structure.
It is the human condition inside the structure.

Case Study
Internal Working Model · 03 · Case Study

Knight Capital Group
August 1, 2012

A study in what happens when a system continues because no one was named, in advance, to stop it.
The Numbers
$440M lost 45 minutes 154 stocks affected $10M per minute Firm did not survive

Knight Capital was one of the largest US equity market makers. On August 1, 2012, a software deployment error activated a dormant trading algorithm. The firm observed the anomaly. Escalation occurred. The system continued executing erroneous orders for 45 minutes while governance attempted to convene.

The process was not absent. The name was.

Timeline

What happened, minute by minute

09:30
Market opens. System activates. A software deployment error on one of eight servers left an old, dormant algorithm active. The system begins executing at volume.
09:31
Anomalous behavior observed. Operators notice unusual order flow. The pattern is not consistent with normal market making. Internal alerts begin appearing.
09:31 — 10:15
Escalation without named stop authority. Calls are made. People are contacted. The question of who can halt the system is not answered by a name. It is answered by a process. The process requires convening. Convening requires time. The system does not wait.
10:15
System manually shut down. After 45 minutes, the trading system is halted. By this point the firm has accumulated positions it cannot sustain.
Following days
$440M loss confirmed. Firm seeks emergency capital. Knight Capital does not survive as an independent entity. It is acquired by Getco in early 2013.
The gap between observation and named stop authority
cost $10 million per minute.
Authority Design Analysis

Where the stop path failed

This was not a technology failure in the ordinary sense. The algorithm executed exactly as it was designed to execute. The deployment process failed to deactivate it on one server. These are recoverable errors in a system with named stop authority.

The critical gap was not detection. Operators detected the anomaly within the first minute. The gap was the distance between detection and halt. That distance was filled by escalation chains, phone calls, and organizational process. None of those are a stop. They are the appearance of governance moving toward a stop.

Detection is not the same as authority.
Escalation is not the same as a stop.

A named individual with pre-documented authority to halt the trading system immediately, without convening, without approval, would have changed the outcome. Not because the error would have been prevented. Because the error would have been contained in the first two minutes instead of the first forty-five.

Lessons for Boards
01

Speed asymmetry is the governance problem

Automated systems execute faster than governance can respond. The governance architecture must account for this explicitly. A committee that convenes in hours cannot govern a system that executes in seconds. Named stop authority is the only mechanism that closes this gap.

02

Observation without authority is not control

Knight Capital's operators observed the anomaly immediately. Observation without a named stop path is not governance. It is documentation of a loss in progress.

03

Process is not a brake

Escalation chains, approval workflows, and risk committees are governance instruments designed for normal operations. They are not designed to halt a system executing at market speed. Treating process as a stop is the architectural error that produced the 45-minute window.

04

The cost of the missing name

No single person was named, in advance, with documented authority to halt the trading system immediately upon detecting anomalous behavior. This is not a speculation about what might have helped. It is a description of the structural gap that converted a software error into a firm-ending event.

05

The question boards must now answer

For every automated system your organization operates: who is named, in advance, to halt it? Not a process. Not a team. Not an escalation path. A person. With a name. With documented authority. With the ability to act before the committee convenes.

The process was not absent.
The name was.
