Human review
is not
refusal authority.

Most modern governance systems already include human oversight: review committees, approval chains, validation steps, monitoring systems, and escalation procedures.

Yet irreversible continuation still happens without meaningful interruption.

A human inside the loop does not guarantee that a human can stop the loop.

Oversight is not
the same as
the authority to interrupt.

The problem is not
the absence of humans.
It is the absence of named authority.

Human review, approval chains, and monitoring systems define who observed, who signed off, who escalated.

They rarely define who, by name, is authorized to refuse continuation once institutional momentum already exists.

As responsibility distributes across functions and systems, refusal authority does not distribute with it.
It disappears.

A human reviews the process.
Named authority to refuse continuation.
Responsibility is distributed across teams.
Distributed responsibility can eliminate refusal authority entirely.
Escalation channels exist.
Escalation delays responsibility. It does not interrupt execution.
The system includes approvals.
Approval is not refusal. It does not guarantee interruption capacity.
Controls focus on policy compliance.
Compliance with policy is not the same as authority before irreversible continuation.
Organizations usually know that execution is happening.
The problem is that interruption carries institutional cost.

Under pressure,
oversight becomes observational.

As systems scale, continuation becomes structurally easier than refusal. The cost of stopping — institutional, political, financial — accumulates.

Under those conditions, human oversight can quietly shift from sovereign authority to passive observation.

A safeguard only becomes real when someone can refuse continuation without ambiguity — and is named in advance to do so.

Who, by name,
is authorized to refuse?
