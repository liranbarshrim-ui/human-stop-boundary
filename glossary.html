Decision Authority Research

Defined Terms

A working vocabulary for the question DAR asks. The terms, distinctions, and test used to examine refusal authority.

DAR functions as a mirror, not a mandate.

It does not determine whether an organization should stop. It reveals whether authority to stop and authority to release continuation have been intentionally defined.

Core Question

Before an irreversible action proceeds, who is authorized to stop or release continuation — by name?

Irreversible Commitment 01

The point at which a decision or action can no longer be undone, recalled, or meaningfully corrected. Once crossed, consequences proceed independent of intent.

DAR is concerned specifically with the moment before this threshold. What happens after is a different problem.

Trigger Authority 02

A named role, function, regulation, or operational mechanism that defines in advance the conditions under which interruption, review, refusal, or release becomes required.

Trigger Authority answers a prior question: who defined the threshold that makes stopping relevant? Without a defined threshold, refusal authority may never become operational — because no moment is recognized as requiring it.

Trigger Authority is distinct from Stop Authority. A threshold can be defined without a named individual authorized to act on it. And a named individual can hold stop authority without any pre-defined threshold that activates it.

DAR does not determine when stopping is required. It asks whether that determination was made in advance — and by whom.

Control Point 03

A position in a process where review, approval, or documentation occurs. If the person in this role refuses or raises concerns, the process may slow — but continuation remains available without an explicit release from that individual.

Control points generate records. They do not determine whether the process can proceed.

A control point can influence a decision. Stop authority can prevent it.

Stop Authority 04

The structural claim that a named individual holds the power to block continuation. If that person refuses, the process cannot proceed without an override — and that override must itself be attributed to a named decision-maker.

Stop authority is not seniority. It is not the authority to recommend stopping. It is the intended design condition in which continuation is blocked until someone, by name, releases it.

Stop Authority describes what the structure is designed to do. Whether it actually functions that way under real conditions is the question Effective Authority answers.

Effective Authority 05

The empirical test for whether Stop Authority functions in practice. DAR does not recognize declared authority — only effective authority.

The test is binary and must be answerable before the event: if the identified party says no, does continuation stop?

If yes — Effective Authority exists. If no — the position may not function as Stop Authority in practice, regardless of its title. Authority that can routinely disappear under pressure may not function as Stop Authority in practice.

Continuation can proceed through escalation, hierarchy, exception, or changed context — if any of these paths remain open, Effective Authority is absent.

DAR examines whether continuation remains a choice — or has quietly become the default.

The L1/L2/L3 taxonomy describes degrees of refusal authority. Effective Authority remains a separate binary test.

Operative Test

If the identified party says no, does continuation stop?

Yes → Effective Authority exists
No → Control Point only — not Stop Authority
Interruption 06

The act of stopping continuation before an irreversible commitment is reached. Interruption requires both the recognition that a threshold has been met and the authority to act on that recognition.

Escalation is not interruption. Documentation is not interruption. A warning that is acknowledged but does not stop the decision is not interruption.

Interruption creates a boundary. Escalation creates visibility. A system can have extensive visibility into risk and still lack any mechanism by which that visibility becomes a stop.

Orphaned Responsibility 07

A condition in which accountability for a decision exists in principle but is held by no one in particular. Everyone reviewed. Everyone approved. No one stopped. After the fact, no individual carries the decision.

Orphaned responsibility may emerge when systems are designed around approval while leaving refusal authority undefined.

The goal is not to prevent failure. The goal is to prevent orphaned responsibility.

Potential Secondary Effect

On behavior before the decision point

When a specific individual is explicitly authorized to stop an irreversible action, that authority may influence how concerns are raised, discussed, and escalated before the final decision point.

DAR does not depend on this effect. Its purpose is only to determine whether a named stop authority exists at the point of irreversible commitment.

This is an observed tendency, not a guaranteed outcome. DAR makes no claim about organizational culture or behavioral change.
