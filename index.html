A structural question

Who, by name, is authorized to refuse?

Not who reviews. Not who approves. Not who escalates.

Who is named, in advance, and authorized to say: “Not yet.”

Matrix Audit examines whether refusal authority actually binds continuation when a decision approaches an irreversible point.

01 / The question

The difference between seeing risk and stopping action

A system can contain oversight, approval, escalation, monitoring, experts and safety procedures — and still have no person whose refusal is binding.

The test is deliberately narrower than “is there governance?”

If the person responsible says no, can the process continue anyway?
  1. 01Name the person.
  2. 02Identify the process and stop condition.
  3. 03Trace what happens after refusal.
  4. 04Test whether continuation can bypass that refusal.
A control point can influence a decision. A refusal authority can prevent it.
Approval is not refusal.Approval permits a process to begin or continue under defined conditions.
Escalation is not refusal.Escalation transfers a concern. It does not necessarily interrupt continuation.
Oversight is not refusal.Seeing the problem does not establish authority to stop the process.
Presence is not authority.A human in the loop may be present without holding binding stop authority.
02 / Method

DAR is a mirror, not a mandate.

The review does not decide whether an organization should stop. It asks whether the organization has intentionally defined who can stop, when they can stop, and whether continuation can proceed without them.

04 / Limits

What this work does not claim

DAR does not claim that every system can be stopped by a human, that a named authority automatically has real power, or that a framework can control arbitrary AI systems outside its enforcement boundary.

The question remains intentionally structural: where a process is claimed to have a refusal authority, can continuation proceed despite that refusal?

Test one process.
