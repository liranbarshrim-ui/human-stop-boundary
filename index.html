A structural question

Who, by name,
is authorized
to refuse?

Not who reviews. Not who approves. Not who escalates.
Who is named, in advance, and authorized to say: "Not yet."

Many systems do not fail because of a bad decision, but because the moment to say "not yet" quietly disappears.

Most organizations can tell you who approves.
Far fewer can tell you who can refuse.

If nobody can say “not yet,” continuation quietly becomes the decision.

Without "not yet,"
continuation quietly becomes the decision.
Why the question is difficult to answer

Most governance structures define who approves. Fewer define who is authorized to refuse — before continuation acquires momentum.

Under pressure, approval scales. Refusal rarely does.

Expertise can be collective. Refusal authority rarely is.
Oversight is not refusal authority. Escalation is not refusal authority. Approval is not refusal.

And even when refusal authority is assigned, it can drift. The person remains. The authority quietly does not.

Who, by name,
is authorized to refuse
in your organization?
Before continuation acquires momentum.

If no one is named in advance, someone will assume the refusal belongs to someone else.

By the time that assumption is tested, someone may realize that the refusal belonged to them.

Why the question is urgent

Irreversible actions increasingly occur before responsibility becomes visible. Continuation can acquire momentum before interruption becomes explicit.

The question is not whether a decision can be made.
The question is who, by name, can still interrupt it.
