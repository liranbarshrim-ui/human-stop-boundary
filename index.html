Liran Bar-Shrim Correspondence

Approval is
not refusal.

A system can contain oversight, human review, escalation, and approval —

and still leave refusal authority unclear in practice.

Everyone reviewed it.
No one refused.

Sometimes, the missing authority is simply the authority to say: "not yet."

No one refused.
So continuation
became automatic.

Oversight is defined.
Refusal authority often is not.

AI systems, automated execution, and distributed approval chains increasingly separate the moment of continuation from any named individual authorized to refuse continuation.

Observation is not authority.
Escalation alone does not create refusal authority.
Approval is not refusal.

Most governance structures define who approves.
Fewer define explicitly who is authorized to refuse — and under what conditions.

As responsibility distributes across functions and systems, refusal authority becomes harder to identify in practice.

The question is never whether the system can continue.
The question is whether anyone inside it
is authorized to refuse.

A committee can gather expertise. That is not the same as someone authorized to stop.

Not who knows more.
Who is authorized to stop when the answer is still unclear.
When continuation remains easier than refusal,
refusal becomes theoretical.

If this question feels difficult to answer in practice, ambiguity may already be shaping continuation.

Refusal remains visible.
But no longer practical.

Systems do not remove judgment directly. They compress time, options, incentives, and uncertainty until continuation becomes the default.

Accountability remains visible.
Refusal becomes procedural.

In practice, continuation often becomes easier than refusal.
That someone needs more than a title.
They need refusal authority assigned in advance —
before pressure, ambiguity, or organizational reinterpretation emerge.
The appointment is the protection.
DAR does not ask who will recognize the moment.
It asks who is already bound before pressure exists.

One structural question.

No institution behind it.
Just the question itself.

Who, by name,
is authorized to refuse?

Who is authorized to say: "not yet"?

If it cannot wait,
it is no longer a decision.
It is momentum.

A deeper examination of refusal authority and irreversible decisions.

DAR does not guarantee refusal. It identifies who should be authorized to refuse, before the question becomes unanswerable.

It does not resolve organizational culture, commercial pressure, or information failures. Those remain separate problems. This one is structural.

Before continuation becomes irreversible,
does refusal still exist?

If the answer cannot be clearly given,
that is where the conversation begins.

DAR examines who is explicitly authorized to refuse continuation before it becomes irreversible.
