Interruption Logic

Systems optimize continuity.

Interruption appears as resistance.

Most operational systems are designed to preserve flow, reduce friction, and maintain execution continuity.

The more continuity becomes institutionalized, the harder interruption becomes psychologically and operationally.

Continuation feels natural
because systems are designed to continue.
Continuity Bias

Execution momentum accumulates legitimacy over time.

Approvals accumulate. Validation accumulates. Operational confidence accumulates.

As institutional confidence increases, interruption begins to appear abnormal rather than protective.

The system no longer asks: “Should continuation proceed?”

It asks: “Why would anyone interrupt?”

Institutional Pressure

Interruption carries visible cost.
Continuation often hides cost until later.

Stopping introduces delay, friction, escalation, exposure, political consequences, and financial pressure.

Continuation preserves operational smoothness in the present moment.

Because interruption cost appears immediately, systems naturally drift toward continuation.

Silence inside a running system
is usually interpreted as approval.
Interruption Layer

DAR treats interruption as legitimate system behavior.

The purpose is not permanent stopping.

The purpose is preserving the ability to interrupt continuation before irreversible exposure becomes normalized.

Without interruption legitimacy, continuation becomes procedural default.

A system that cannot tolerate interruption
cannot safely govern irreversible execution.
