What this series observes

Each case is drawn from the public record. No insider knowledge is claimed. No fault is attributed to named individuals.

What the public record makes visible, across three separate domains, is a recurring structural relationship between continuity architecture and interruption architecture.

In each case: the path toward continuation was faster, more automated, or more institutionally embedded than the path toward interruption. In automated environments, responsibility can drift faster than authority is named. And in each case, the public record does not identify a named individual whose explicit release was required before continuation could proceed under conditions of material uncertainty.

The recurring structure
What appeared in each case
Oversight. Process. Escalation channels. Approval authority.
What the public record does not show
A named individual with prior, explicit commitment to refuse continuation under uncertainty.
The structural relationship
The continuation path was faster or more embedded than the interruption path.
At what point did continuation cease to require explicit re-authorization from a named individual — and who, by name, held that authority before the event?

These observations are drawn from publicly available post-incident analyses, regulatory findings, congressional testimony, and published reporting. DAR makes no claim to insider knowledge of any of the organizations examined. No fault or negligence is attributed to named individuals. The governance gap identified in each case is structural and observable from the public record alone.
