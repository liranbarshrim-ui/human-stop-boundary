Authority Reconstruction · #03

Knight Capital
The Interruption Window

09:30 — 09:58 ET · August 1, 2012

This is not a reconstruction of the software failure.
It is a reconstruction of interruption authority under unresolved uncertainty.

01 — Activation
09:30 ET · August 1, 2012

Market opens.
Execution begins.

Knight Capital Group was one of the largest equity market makers in the United States, responsible for approximately 10% of all U.S. equity trading volume.

At 09:30, the market opened. Knight's SMARS routing system began processing incoming orders. On seven of eight servers, the new deployment functioned correctly. On the eighth, dormant legacy code had been reactivated during the prior week's deployment.

Execution was live. The anomaly was already in motion.

02 — Signal
09:30 — ~09:38 ET

Abnormal behavior
becomes visible.

Within minutes of market open, Knight's systems began generating orders at abnormal volume. The firm was buying high and selling low across 154 stocks simultaneously, at a rate and pattern inconsistent with any intended trading strategy.

External market participants noticed the anomaly before Knight's internal systems produced a clear diagnostic. Other firms contacted Knight. Market activity in affected stocks surged.

Documentary Basis

Knight's executions constituted more than 50% of trading volume in certain stocks during this period, causing price movements exceeding 10% in 37 securities.

Source: SEC Order — Knight Capital Group, Inc., October 16, 2013
03 — Escalation Under Uncertainty
~09:38 ET

Source unclear.
Execution persists.

Knight personnel recognized that something was wrong. The source of the anomaly remained undiagnosed.

In an attempt to resolve the issue, engineers decided to revert all servers to the prior software version. This decision — made under pressure without confirmed diagnosis — had the opposite effect. The legacy code that had caused the anomaly was now deployed across all eight servers. Execution volume increased.

Documented incident-response procedures were insufficient to rapidly identify and interrupt abnormal execution while the source remained unclear.

Documentary Basis

Knight had no written incident-response procedures for this type of event. After concluding the issue was with the new code, engineers reverted all servers to the prior version — which contained the defective Power Peg code — causing the anomaly to expand across all eight servers.

Source: SEC Order — Knight Capital Group, Inc., October 16, 2013
04 — Interruption Lag
09:38 — 09:58 ET

Execution persisted
under unresolved uncertainty.

The anomaly was visible.
Interruption lagged understanding.

Execution persisted while the source of the anomaly remained unresolved. Documented interruption pathways under uncertainty remained unclear in practice.

The anomaly was visible.
Interruption lagged understanding.

By 09:58, engineers identified the root cause and shut down SMARS. By that point, over 4 million executions had occurred across 154 stocks. Losses exceeded $460 million.

05 — The Interruption Question

Once abnormal execution
became visible but undiagnosed,
who carried interruption authority?

This is not a question about intent, competence, or engineering judgment.

It is a structural question:

Who carried interruption — by name?
06 — Structural Observation

What existed.
What remained unclear.
