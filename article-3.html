Ownership · Essay Three On Collective Accountability and the Missing Owner of Interruption

Who Is Actually Expected to Stop?

On Collective Accountability and the Missing Owner of Interruption

Liran Bar-Shrim  ·  May 2026

Organizations may preserve formal authority to interrupt.

They may even preserve the visibility required to recognize when interruption is necessary.

Yet interruption can still fail for a quieter reason:

No specific person understands that stopping belongs to them.

I

The Comfort of Collective Responsibility

After most consequential failures, organizations can identify what happened.

They can trace decisions. They can reconstruct timelines. They can assign accountability in retrospect.

The language of collective responsibility survives intact:

We reviewed it. We escalated it. We followed the process.

This language is not dishonest. In most cases, review did occur. Escalation did happen. Process was followed.

What is less often examined is whether any of this amounted to meaningful interruption — or whether responsibility for stopping was ever clearly assigned to anyone in particular.

Collective responsibility is comfortable precisely because it distributes ownership without concentrating it.

Everyone participates. No one bears singular expectation.

II

Why Escalation Is Not Interruption

Organizations often treat escalation as the governance mechanism for uncertain decisions.

Escalation sounds like governance. It has structure. It has process. It has documentation.

But escalation and interruption are not the same thing.

Escalation routes a concern upward. It distributes the question to a broader audience. It may increase visibility.

But visibility without ownership does not guarantee interruption. It does not, by itself, designate anyone as the person whose specific responsibility it is to say: Not yet.

When escalation is the primary governance mechanism, organizations may create the appearance of meaningful interruption while leaving the actual responsibility for stopping diffuse, unassigned, and ultimately unexercised.

Everyone escalated. No one stopped.

III

The Drift Toward Diffused Accountability

Modern governance structures are designed to distribute responsibility. Committees. Cross-functional approval. Shared ownership. Multi-stakeholder review.

These structures reflect genuine wisdom about the limits of individual judgment. They prevent single points of failure. They incorporate diverse perspectives.

But they may also produce an unintended consequence: the gradual dissolution of singular interruption expectation.

When responsibility is distributed across a committee, no individual member necessarily understands that the obligation to stop belongs specifically to them. When approval requires multiple signatures, no single signatory necessarily bears the expectation that their refusal would halt execution.

Collective governance can preserve accountability in the aggregate while eliminating it at the level of named expectation.

Everyone is responsible. No one is responsible by name.

IV

The Moment Before Failure

There is a specific moment that tends to appear in reconstructions of consequential failures.

Not the moment of catastrophic error. The moment before.

A decision felt uncertain. Several people noticed signals. Concerns existed and were acknowledged. Escalation occurred.

But no single person understood: If this proceeds and causes harm because we failed to stop it, that responsibility is mine.

Without that singular named expectation, the decision continued.

Not because no one cared. Not because process was absent. Not because authority to interrupt did not exist.

But because no one carried the specific, named, operational expectation that stopping was their responsibility to exercise.

The decision felt collectively reviewed. It was individually unowned.
V

The Harder Governance Question

Governance traditionally asks: Who is accountable after failure?

The harder question — the one organizations rarely ask in advance:

Who was explicitly expected to interrupt before failure, by name?

Not a function. Not a committee. Not a process.

A person. Named. Carrying the specific understanding that if continuation becomes inadvisable, stopping belongs to them.

The absence of that named expectation may not be visible until failure makes it apparent.

By then, responsibility will be reconstructed. It was never assigned.
