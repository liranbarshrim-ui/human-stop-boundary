Why DAR Exists

Three different governance failures can produce the same outcome. DAR begins from a simple observation about what meaningful interruption actually requires.

Three conditions. Each can fail independently.
01

Does the person responsible still see enough to know that stopping is necessary?

Mediated systems increasingly filter, compress, and rank information before it reaches formal review. The authority to stop may remain intact while the visibility required to exercise it quietly narrows.

02

Does the person who sees enough actually have the authority to stop?

Visibility and authority are not always held by the same person. The individual closest to the problem may not carry the institutional standing to interrupt. The individual with standing may no longer encounter the problem directly.

03

Does the person with visibility and authority understand that stopping belongs to them — by name?

Even when the first two conditions are met, interruption can fail. When responsibility is distributed across committees, functions, and shared approval processes, no individual necessarily carries the singular expectation that stopping is theirs to exercise.

The structural observation

Meaningful interruption depends on all three conditions. The failure of any one of them may allow continuation to proceed — even when the others remain intact.

The narrower observation

Most organizations can identify visibility, authority, or accountability in isolation.

DAR begins from a narrower observation: interruption depends on all three existing simultaneously.

The absence of any one of them may allow continuation to proceed — even when the others remain fully intact. And the absence is rarely visible until it is too late to correct.

By the time responsibility is reconstructed, it was never assigned.

DAR functions as a mirror, not a mandate. It does not determine whether an organization should stop. It reveals whether authority to stop and authority to release continuation have been intentionally defined.
The question DAR asks

Before an irreversible action proceeds, who is authorized to stop or release continuation — by name?

Not a function. Not a committee. Not a process. A person, named in advance, carrying the specific understanding that if continuation becomes inadvisable, stopping belongs to them.

What DAR does

DAR does not attempt to improve decision quality. It does not determine whether a decision is correct.

The observation that meaningful stopping requires visibility, authority, and ownership to exist simultaneously is what DAR begins from — not what it proves.

It establishes, in advance, who carries the authority to stop or release continuation when uncertainty remains and the decision is approaching irreversibility.

Where the principle already exists

Named stop authority is not a new idea. It already appears in aviation, nuclear operations, and surgical practice — domains where the consequences of unchecked continuation are immediate and irreversible.

In those domains, the person authorized to stop or release continuation is identified before the process begins. Their authority is structural, not situational. Continuation is unavailable without their explicit release.

The question is not whether named stop authority exists.

The question is where organizations believe continuation has become important enough to require it.

Why the question is difficult to answer

Most organizations can describe who approves. Fewer can name, in advance, who is authorized to stop or release continuation.

The difficulty is not procedural. Processes exist. Review occurs. Escalation is possible.

The difficulty is structural: approval scales under pressure. Refusal rarely does. Expertise can be collective. Named stop authority rarely is.

And even when it is assigned, it can drift. The person remains. The authority quietly does not.
