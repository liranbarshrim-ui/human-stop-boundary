# Named Stop Authority — Framework
### Decision Authority Research — Liran Bar-Shrim

---

## The Problem

Most organizations know how to approve decisions.  
Almost none know who can stop them.

Approval workflows, escalation structures, budget authority — these are well-defined.  
The question of who can interrupt momentum immediately, before execution becomes irreversible, is almost never answered with a name.

This is not an oversight. It is architecture.  
Organizations diffuse responsibility to avoid the burden of personal accountability.  
Excuses invoking scale, complexity, or culture are signals of avoidance.

---

## The Core Question

> Who, by name, can stop this decision before it becomes irreversible?

Not a team. Not a function. Not a committee. A person. Named. Accountable. Committed in advance.

If the answer requires meetings, escalation chains, or interpretation — time may already be lost.

---

## When Named Stop Authority Applies

Two conditions must be present simultaneously:

1. **Irreversibility** — the decision, once executed, cannot be undone without disproportionate cost, harm, or loss.
2. **Material uncertainty** — there is meaningful uncertainty about whether execution is correct at the moment of decision.

When both conditions exist, continuation requires a named human release. Without it, the default is stop.

---

## The Accountability Structure

### Stop Authority
Named in advance. Empowered to halt continuation when trigger conditions are met.  
Protected from retaliation for good-faith invocation.  
Cannot be the release authority.

### Hold Authority
Maintains the suspension while the governance response is organized.  
Accountable for the duration of the hold and for preventing circumvention.  
Distinct from both stop and release authority.

### Release Authority
Named in advance. Empowered to lift the stop following documented governance review.  
Release requires written rationale.  
Cannot be the individual who invoked the stop.

### Escalation Path
If no response arrives within a defined window, the hold escalates automatically.  
Three levels named in advance: primary, secondary, board-level.  
Silence triggers escalation. Silence is not consent.

---

## Mandatory Response Architecture

A hold without mandatory response is a slow override by silence.

Between Stop and Release there is a window. Most organizations leave this window unowned.  
No one is required to respond. No clock is running.  
The hold dissolves under organizational pressure without formal release.

**What must be defined in advance:**
- Maximum response window before escalation
- Who must respond (not whoever is available — a named individual)
- What constitutes a valid response (documented position, not acknowledgment)
- Default state if no response: hold remains active

---

## Why Stop Authority Fails Even When It Exists

### 1. Incentive structure punishes stopping
The person with stop authority owns a number. Stopping damages it. No threat is needed. The KPI structure cancels the authority automatically.

### 2. The stop is survivable once, not twice
One punished stop sends a signal. Everyone with similar authority reads it. The second invocation never comes.

### 3. Override is informal and costless
A senior leader expresses displeasure. No formal override occurs. The stop authority reverses position. The structure survived. The authority did not.

### 4. Protection is promised but not structural
Verbal commitments and cultural norms are not governance. When the moment arrives, they evaporate under pressure.

### 5. Oversight belongs to the person who loses most from the stop
The supervisor asks how quickly it can be reversed, not whether the stop was justified.

---

## Minimum Viable Implementation

Five elements. Organizations with all five have a real stop. Missing any one: they do not.

1. **Named person** — one individual, by name, assigned to a defined decision category
2. **Trigger classes** — written specification of conditions under which stop authority applies
3. **Temporary hold period** — maximum duration before mandatory escalation
4. **Named release authority** — a second individual, distinct from the stop authority, with documented release power
5. **Audit log** — every invocation, hold, release, and escalation recorded and reviewable independently

---

## What This Is Not

Named Stop Authority is not day-to-day governance.  
It is not a veto over ordinary decisions.  
It is not the same as the release authority.  
It is not a framework, policy, or checklist.

It is a condition: the pre-identified assignment of a specific individual who holds protected, documented, and enforceable power to halt a defined category of irreversible decision.

---

## The Upstream Effect

Named Stop Authority does not derive its value from frequency of invocation.  
An authority invoked constantly has been misdesigned.

The structural value lies elsewhere: in what the existence of a named stop does to behavior upstream of any decision.

When individuals know that a specific person holds documented authority to halt a consequential action, the decision environment changes before any decision is made.  
Weak assumptions are challenged earlier.  
Corners are cut less often.  
Documentation improves not because it is mandated — but because people anticipate scrutiny.

> A real brake changes driving even when untouched.

---

## Relation to Existing Governance Instruments

| Instrument | What it answers |
|---|---|
| RACI | Who owns the decision |
| KPI | What moved |
| Audit | What happened |
| Policy | What was allowed |
| Human-in-the-loop | A person was present |
| **Named Stop Authority** | **Who had the authority to stop it in real time** |

Named Stop Authority does not replace these systems.  
It asks the question none of them ask.
