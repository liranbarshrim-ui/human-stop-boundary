# Named Stop Authority — Framework
### Decision Accountability Review — Liran Bar-Shrim

---

## The Problem

Most organizations know how to approve decisions.  
The harder question is who can stop them within the process boundary being governed.

Approval workflows, escalation structures, budget authority — these are well-defined.  
The question of who can interrupt momentum immediately, before execution becomes difficult or impossible to reverse, is often left without a clearly evidenced answer.

This is a structural question, not a claim about every organization or every system. The purpose is to identify where responsibility and practical interruption authority diverge.

---

## The Core Question

> Who, by name, can refuse continuation before it becomes difficult or impossible to reverse?

Not merely a team, function, or committee. Where individual accountability is claimed, identify the accountable person and the authority boundary attached to them.

If the answer requires meetings, escalation chains, or interpretation, the review should test whether those mechanisms actually interrupt continuation in time.

---

## When Named Stop Authority Applies

Two conditions are especially relevant:

1. **Irreversibility** — the decision, once executed, cannot be undone without disproportionate cost, harm, or loss.
2. **Material uncertainty** — there is meaningful uncertainty about whether execution is correct at the moment of decision.

Where both conditions exist and an organization has defined a human refusal authority, continuation should be tested against that authority. A declared default state is meaningful only if the process and enforcement boundary actually make it binding.

---

## The Accountability Structure

### Stop Authority
Named in advance. Required to halt or refuse continuation when defined trigger conditions are met.  
Protected from retaliation for good-faith invocation where the organization has made such protection part of its governance design.  
Should not also be the release authority where separation is required by the design.

### Hold Authority
Maintains a suspension while the governance response is organized.  
Accountable for the duration of the hold and for preventing circumvention where the process assigns that responsibility.  
Distinct from stop and release authority when the design requires separation.

### Release Authority
Named in advance where applicable. Empowered to lift the stop following documented governance review.  
Release should carry a written rationale where the process requires one.  
Should be distinct from the individual who invoked the stop where separation is required.

### Escalation Path
If no response arrives within a defined window, the hold may escalate according to the process design.  
Primary, secondary, and board-level paths can be named in advance.  
Silence is not automatically consent unless the governing process explicitly makes it so.

---

## Mandatory Response Architecture

A hold without mandatory response can become a slow override by silence.

Between Stop and Release there is a window. Governance should define who owns that window, what response is required, and what happens if the response does not arrive.

**What can be defined in advance:**
- Maximum response window before escalation
- Who must respond (a named individual or explicitly defined role)
- What constitutes a valid response (documented position, not acknowledgment)
- Default state if no response: for example, hold remains active

The review tests whether these rules are actually followed and enforceable within the claimed boundary.

---

## Why Stop Authority Fails Even When It Exists

### 1. Incentive structure can punish stopping
The person with stop authority may own a metric or objective that makes stopping costly. No explicit threat is required for practical authority to weaken.

### 2. One punished invocation can change future behavior
A single visible consequence can signal to others that invocation carries unacceptable cost. The structural authority may remain on paper while practical use declines.

### 3. Override can be informal and costless
A senior leader may express displeasure without a formal override. The stop authority can reverse position under pressure. The documented structure survives; practical authority may not.

### 4. Protection can be promised but not structural
Verbal commitments and cultural norms are evidence, but they are not by themselves proof of enforceable protection.

### 5. Oversight can conflict with the stop function
Where the person evaluating a stop also bears the strongest incentive to continue, the independence of the refusal function should be tested rather than assumed.

---

## Minimum Viable Implementation

Five elements provide a useful testable structure. Their presence is not, by itself, proof that a stop is effective; the implementation and evidence must also be tested.

1. **Named person** — one individual, by name, assigned to a defined decision category
2. **Trigger classes** — written specification of conditions under which stop authority applies
3. **Temporary hold period** — defined duration or response rule before mandatory escalation
4. **Named release authority** — a second individual, distinct from the stop authority, with documented release power where separation is required
5. **Audit log** — every invocation, hold, release, and escalation recorded and reviewable independently

---

## What This Is Not

Named Stop Authority is not day-to-day governance.  
It is not a veto over ordinary decisions.  
It is not the same as the release authority.  
It is not a guarantee that a named person can control actions outside the governed boundary.

It is a condition: the pre-identified assignment of a specific individual who holds documented authority to halt or refuse a defined category of consequential decision, together with an enforcement mechanism capable of making that authority binding where claimed.

---

## The Upstream Effect

Named Stop Authority does not derive its value from frequency of invocation.  
An authority invoked constantly may indicate that the process or threshold has been poorly designed.

The structural value may lie elsewhere: in what the existence of a named stop does to behavior upstream of a decision.

When individuals know that a specific person holds documented authority to halt a consequential action, the decision environment can change before any decision is made.  
Weak assumptions may be challenged earlier.  
Corners may be cut less often.  
Documentation may improve because people anticipate scrutiny.

> A real brake changes driving even when untouched.

This is a governance hypothesis to be tested, not a universal causal guarantee.

## Relation to Existing Governance Instruments

| Instrument | What it answers |
|---|---|
| RACI | Who owns the decision |
| KPI | What moved |
| Audit | What happened |
| Policy | What was allowed |
| Human-in-the-loop | A person was present |
| **Named Stop Authority** | **Who was designated to refuse continuation in the defined process** |

Named Stop Authority does not replace these systems.  
It asks a narrower question about interruption authority and whether the claimed authority is actually binding within its boundary.
