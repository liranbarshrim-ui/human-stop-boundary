Knowledge Has No Death Rules

Why long-refuted claims stay alive, and who has the authority to say stop.

By Liran Bar-Shrim · July 2026

I was born in January 1985.

That same year, the journal Nature published the experiment that was supposed to kill astrology. Shawn Carlson, a physicist at Berkeley, did what almost no one had bothered to do before: he gave astrologers the conditions they themselves had asked for. Experienced astrologers, full partners in designing the experiment, approved the test as fair in advance. They received natal charts and tried to match them to real personality profiles. If the sky holds information about human beings, this is where it should have surfaced.

The result was chance level. Exactly.

Forty years have passed. Astrology has not heard of its own death. It thrives in apps, in horoscope columns, in dating conversations. I know, because that is where I meet it: a woman I was seeing explained to me that I am "cold", because Capricorn. The experiment born the same year I was did not help me with her in the slightest. The refutation was right there: complete, signed, published in the world's most prestigious scientific journal, and utterly powerless.

And in the interest of precision: Carlson's experiment did not end the argument either. Over the years, critiques and reanalyses have been published, some by supporters of astrology, claiming flaws in the design or in the interpretation of the data. I make no attempt to adjudicate between these claims here. But notice the fact itself: four decades after publication, there exists no procedure by which the dispute over the experiment's standing comes to an end. That, too, is part of the phenomenon this essay sets out to explain.

Human beings die. Companies dissolve. States vanish. Stars collapse. Only a claim about the world is capable of something that has no parallel anywhere else in existence: to be refuted, to be documented as refuted, and to go on living for thousands of years.

Ideas have no death certificate. The question of this essay is why.

The phenomenonKnowledge knows how to be born. It does not know how to die.

Every institutional knowledge community has admission rules. Orderly, detailed, enforced. How a claim enters the scientific literature: peer review, replication, statistics. How a fact enters a textbook, an encyclopedia, a court ruling. Humanity has been posting guards at the entrance gates of knowledge for thousands of years, and it invests in refining them to this day.

Now ask the opposite question: what are the exit rules? What is the procedure by which a refuted claim leaves circulation? Who is authorized to declare it, when, and what is the claim's standing the day after?

The question sounds symmetrical to the first one. It is not. The first has answers in every field; the second has almost no answer anywhere.

The phenomenon

Every system has rules that let claims in. Almost no system has rules that end their lives.

That is the phenomenon, and it is far broader than astrology. The fabricated claim linking vaccines to autism survived the retraction of the paper that spawned it, the revocation of its author's license, and the massive studies that examined millions of children. The myth that we use ten percent of our brains has survived every brain scan showing the opposite. These are not isolated cases of human stubbornness. This is the pattern.

The standard explanations turn to psychology: confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, group identity. All of them are correct, and all of them answer a different question: why the individual struggles to give up a belief. They do not explain why human knowledge as a system, with its institutions, journals, and standards, lacks a procedure for removing a dead claim from circulation.

A systemic phenomenon requires a structural explanation. I would like to propose one.

The explanationRefutation has no name

Since 2024 I have been investigating one question inside organizations: when a dangerous decision is being made, who holds the authority to stop it. Not who is responsible, not who approves. Who can say "no" in a way that binds. The question grew out of daily work with artificial intelligence systems, and you will see later why it emerged from there of all places. The finding that recurs in almost every organization is a distinction that sounds technical and turns out to be fateful:

The distinction

There is a difference between advisory authority and binding authority.

Advisory authority says "this is dangerous", and the process may proceed. Binding authority says "no", and the process stops, and no one is authorized to override. Organizations meticulously document who approves; few can say who is entitled to refuse in a way that binds. And the difference always reveals itself at the same moment: after the disaster, when it turns out someone said "no", and their word was a recommendation.

Apply that distinction to human knowledge, and the phenomenon acquires its mechanism:

The mechanism

Scientific refutation is advisory authority. It has no binding authority, and it never has had.

Economists have a name for claims that survive their own refutation, zombie ideas in John Quiggin's phrase; what follows is an attempt to explain the mechanism that keeps them undead. When Carlson published in Nature, he told the world "this is wrong", and stopped nothing. Publication lets claims into knowledge; it has no power to remove them from it. The marketplace of ideas, unlike any real marketplace, is one where a product that has failed inspection goes on being sold forever, because the market has no enforcement agency.

Ptolemy is the proof at the largest scale. His geocentric model organized the picture of the universe for 1,400 years, not because no one suspected it, but because there existed no body authorized to stop it. And when Copernicus and Galileo finally refuted Ptolemy's astronomy, the strangest thing of all happened: his astronomy died. And his astrology, built on exactly the same erroneous sky, carried on as if nothing had happened, and it is running to this day.

How can the same refutation have killed half the doctrine and missed the other half? The answer arrives through the cases that seem to contradict the thesis.

The objectionBut some claims did die

Every theory must face the cases that appear to refute it. And the cases here are famous. Phlogiston, the imaginary substance that was supposed to explain combustion, died. The luminiferous aether, the medium through which light waves were supposed to travel, died. Geocentrism died. No chemist assumes phlogiston, no physicist computes the aether, no astronomer places the Earth at the center.

If knowledge has no death mechanism, who killed them?

Check where they died, and you will find the answer. They did not die "in human knowledge." They died inside the narrowest communities history has known (professional chemistry, physics, and astronomy), which are, not coincidentally, almost the only spaces that built for themselves what everyone else lacks: an institutional procedure that changes a claim's standing in response to evidence. Journals that stop accepting. Curricula that remove. Standards that forbid assuming the dead claim in a calculation. In these communities, a refuted claim is not merely "wrong". It loses its license.

And now the decisive evidence, the one that turns the objection into the thesis's control group: the very same claims that died inside these communities live and flourish outside them. The aether died in physics, and lives happily in the New Age world, dressed up as "energies" and "frequencies" filling space. Geocentrism died in astronomy, and lives in astrology, which goes on computing your fate by a sky that revolves around the Earth. One claim, two spaces: in the space that has an ending procedure, it was buried. In the space that has none, it is immortal.

This also solves the Ptolemy riddle. Copernicus's refutation was not stronger in astronomy and weaker in astrology. It was identical. What differed was the space it landed in.

And the thesis, which began as a claim about failure, becomes here a claim that predicts. In its general form:

The prediction

The fate of a refuted claim does not depend on the strength of the refutation, but on the existence of an institutional procedure for changing its standing in the space where it lives.

Give me a field, tell me whether such a procedure exists in it, and I will tell you whether claims die there, and at what rate. The prediction can be tested on any ongoing knowledge crisis; the replication crisis in the social sciences, unfolding as we speak, is one living laboratory among many.

The rival theoryMaybe it's all incentives?

This thesis competes with at least one serious alternative explanation. Here it is at its best.

According to incentive theory, refuted claims survive when someone profits from them, and die when the error starts costing money. Astrology survived because it is an industry: horoscopes sell, apps raise capital, and none of the beneficiaries wants it dead. Newton's physics is carefully managed because a miscalculated bridge collapses, and the collapse costs lives and money. Engineering cannot afford dead claims; the horoscope column can. No need for "death rules," says the theory. Just follow the money and the cost.

The explanation is elegant, and it does cover a large share of the cases. But there is a test that separates the two theories: a case where incentives predict one outcome and institutions predict another. And it exists.

Bloodletting.

For roughly two thousand years, bloodletting was the most common medical treatment in the West. Now consider the incentives: they were the strongest possible, pointing in exactly the right directions. The patient wanted to live. The physician wanted his patients to recover; his livelihood and his name depended on it. No one profited from patients dying. If incentives kill wrong claims, bloodletting should have died within a generation.

It lived two thousand years. Physicians bled their patients (and themselves, and their own children) with pure intentions and lethal results, because the intensity of wanting the truth does not generate a procedure for finding it.

And the precise history of its end is more instructive still, because it demonstrates the two stages of the thesis separately. In the 1830s, the French physician Pierre Louis supplied the refutation. His "numerical method", a systematic comparison of outcomes across groups of patients and a milestone on the road to modern epidemiology, showed that the confidence placed in bloodletting was not supported by the facts. And what happened? Exactly what happened to Carlson: almost nothing. Many physicians went on bleeding patients for another thirty years and more. The refutation existed; the claim's standing did not change.

Bloodletting's standing truly changed only later in the century, as something matured that had not existed in the previous two thousand years: institutional medicine (journals, uniform training, standards of care), a community in which evidence changes standing in a way that binds. The incentive had been constant for two millennia. The refutation alone was not enough, as refutations never are. What decided the matter was the institution.

The separating test

In the case where the two theories predict different outcomes, the facts decided in favor of institutions. Incentives explain why people want a claim to lose its standing. They do not explain how that happens.

Sharpening the claimWhat a "death rule" is, and what it is not

A moment of care is required here, because the predictable criticism is clear: claims are not organisms. They have no "life" and no "death." People simply use them, or stop.

The criticism is correct, which is why my claim is more modest than the metaphor suggests, and more precise than it. I am not arguing that claims literally die. I am arguing that knowledge communities either have, or lack, a death rule: an institutional mechanism that changes the epistemic or normative standing of a claim in response to new evidence. Without erasing it, without forbidding anyone to think about it, without censoring. Only determining, with authority, what may be assumed on its basis from now on. And I choose the word "death" not because claims die, but because the decisive question is not whether a claim is true. It is whether a community has stopped treating it as a valid basis for new knowledge. That, and not its truth, is the moment a claim stops operating in the world.

A warning is also required: the existence of a death rule is no guarantee of truth. History knows institutions that changed the standing of claims wrongly, and sometimes violently. The thesis here does not address how such a rule ought to be exercised, or by whom, but a question prior to that one: in which spaces does a mechanism for changing standing exist at all, and what happens to claims in the spaces where it does not.

And the most beautiful demonstration that a death rule is not an execution is precisely the case where the refuted claim remained in use: Newton.

Newtonian physics was refuted. Einstein showed it is wrong at its foundations: time is not absolute, space is not a fixed stage. And yet every bridge, every satellite, every car is computed today by Newton. Is engineering astrology? No, because the scientific community neither "killed" Newton nor ignored the refutation. It exercised a death rule in its fullest gentleness: it changed his standing. "An approximate model. Valid at low velocities and in weak gravitational fields. Outside that domain, forbidden." The GPS in your pocket enforces that assignment dozens of times a day, correcting Newton by Einstein.

That is the entire difference between a managed error and an unmanaged one. Newton is an error with a defined standing, with boundaries, with a body authorized to say where it ends. Astrology is an error that no one was ever authorized to bound. Both were refuted. One of them is managed.

And from here follows the essay's boundary of application, which I place deliberately: every example here is an empirical claim, a claim about the world that evidence can decide. I claim nothing about moral, political, or religious ideas, where the very question "who is authorized to end this" is a different question, and a more dangerous one. The precise claim is this: claims about the world have no universal mechanism that ends their standing. Only local islands that built one for themselves. Less dramatic. Much harder to knock down.

A thought experimentThe day after the refutation

Suppose tomorrow morning a discovery is published that refutes some foundation of our picture of the world, something on the order of finding that a central model in medicine is wrong.

Inside the professional community, the process is known: journals will publish, clinical guidelines will be updated, committees will convene, and within a few years the old model will receive a new standing. Slow, cumbersome, but it exists.

Now step outside and ask: who decides when high-school textbooks change? When journalists stop citing the old model as fact? When it disappears from the Wikipedias of small languages? And who decides when artificial intelligence systems, which learned the old model from millions of documents, stop serving it to whoever asks?

There is no answer. Not a bad answer. There is no procedure to which the question is even addressed. And that is the thesis, presented not as an argument but as an experience: the moment a claim crosses the boundary of the community that produced it, it enters a space that contains no death rule at all. And in that space, most human beings live, most of the time.

This is also why the global war on misinformation is fighting on the wrong front. The prevailing assumption is that refutations are in short supply: more fact-checking, more outreach. But refutations exist in abundance; Carlson has existed for forty years. What does not exist is what was never built: procedures that turn a refutation from information into standing.

And here the arc that opened this essay closes. The question reached me through work with artificial intelligence, because there it stops being theoretical: AI systems today enlist the whole of human knowledge, including every claim that was refuted and never removed from circulation, and return it to the world in fluent prose, at a scale Ptolemy never dreamed of. A machine trained on an archive that does not distinguish a living claim from a dead one will serve both with equal devotion. The question "who is authorized to change the standing of a claim" has ceased to be philosophical. It has become an engineering question, and an urgent one.

ClosingHow do you end a falsehood

Humanity has invested thousands of years in one question: how to discover truth. Aristotle's logic, Descartes's doubt, the experimental method, Popper's falsification, all of them are hurdles we placed at the entrance gate of knowledge, and all of them serve the same question.

Almost no one has asked the second question: how do you end a falsehood.

These are not the same question. The first concerns birth; the second concerns the standing of what has already been born and turned out to be wrong. And the gap between the investment in the first and the neglect of the second is, if this essay is right, one of the deep structures shaping what humanity knows, and what it cannot manage to stop "knowing."

This essay, too, of course, has no binding authority. It is advisory authority only, and by its own thesis it will stop nothing: not the horoscopes, not even the stigma about Capricorns. But in exactly this it differs from what it describes: a myth never points at the limit of its own power. A system that has lost the ability to say "I may be wrong" (the one Ptolemy built, the one every conspiracy theory builds) is a system that hides from itself the question of who is entitled to end it. An essay that defines its boundaries of application, presents its rival theory at its best, and tries to refute itself before the reader does, applies to itself, in miniature, the rule it is asking for.

In nature, things end without a signature. Stars collapse, species go extinct, and no one signs the certificate. But claims about the world are not nature. They are our own making. And of our own making we may, and must, demand what I demand of every organization I examine:

Refusal must have a name.

Liran Bar-Shrim is an independent researcher and the founder of DAR (Decision Authority Research), a framework for examining stop authority in irreversible organizational processes.
